At the edge of a quiet town, a small library opened its doors before the morning traffic began.
The first visitor carried a notebook, a reusable cup, and a list of questions about the building.
She wanted to understand how a place filled with old books could prepare for a changing future.
The librarian suggested that they begin with the reading room, where sunlight fell across a long wooden table.
Every chair had been repaired rather than replaced, and the lamps used less electricity than the ones installed ten years earlier.
A notice beside the entrance explained that the library now offered both printed books and downloadable audio.
For some readers, listening made a difficult chapter easier to follow without taking away the pleasure of the story.
Others preferred to switch between the page and the narrator while traveling, cooking, or resting their eyes.
The visitor asked whether a computer could provide that narration without sending the text to a distant server.
The librarian brought out a laptop and opened an article about the history of the local railway.
Instead of preparing the entire recording first, the program divided the article into individual sentences.
It generated the opening sentence, began playing it, and worked on the next sentence at the same time.
The important question was not simply whether one sentence could be spoken quickly.
The program needed to maintain enough finished audio to keep the listener from waiting between sentences.
They imagined the waiting audio as water in a tank, with a tap filling it and a small outlet draining it.
If the tap supplied water faster than the outlet removed it, the level would gradually rise.
If a difficult sentence took unusually long, the stored audio would give the computer time to catch up.
The visitor wrote down three measurements: the initial wait, the smallest reserve, and the number of interruptions.
She also noted that a reserve naturally falls to zero when the final sentence finishes playing.
That normal ending should not be mistaken for a failure in the middle of a reading session.
Outside, a delivery driver unloaded twelve boxes while a bus stopped across the street.
Inside, the laptop continued reading without needing to prepare a separate audio file for every sentence.
The article described a station built in 1924 and a renovation that cost 125,000 dollars.
It included a timetable, several unfamiliar place names, and a quotation from an engineer.
Those details made the passage more demanding than a collection of short and familiar greetings.
The librarian explained that a useful experiment should resemble the work the finished application would actually do.
A quick demonstration could prove that the pieces connected, but a longer session could reveal problems hidden by an average.
They agreed to keep the voice and speaking speed unchanged so that comparisons would remain meaningful.
They would also distinguish measurements made by the program from judgments made by a person listening.
A software counter could report an empty buffer, but it could not decide whether a voice sounded pleasant.
Likewise, a smooth recording would not prove that the same settings would work on every other computer.
By the time the article reached its final paragraph, several more visitors had entered the reading room.
One borrowed a history book, another returned a map, and a child asked where the astronomy shelf had moved.
The visitor closed her notebook and thanked the librarian for turning an abstract performance question into something observable.
Before leaving, she listened to the last sentence and waited for the room to become quiet again.
